# Decision log

## Decided

| # | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D1 | Top-down 2D on a grid | Least work, and the gameplay doesn't need 3D |
| D2 | PixiJS v8 rather than Phaser | Only a renderer is needed; physics and scene management would go unused, and PixiJS batches sprites more lightly and quickly |
| D3 | The simulation runs in a Web Worker | The main thread only renders and handles input, so the game doesn't stutter |
| D4 | Integer-only, deterministic simulation | Reliable saves, replay debugging, room for multiplayer |
| D5 | Belts are transport lines with gap arrays | The only approach proven to scale |
| D6 | TypeScript first, with data laid out as TypedArrays | Fast iteration, cheap to move to WASM if needed |
| D7 | v1 is single-player with no combat or fluids | Keeps the focus on the core loop |
| D8 | Original names and art | Avoids copyright problems |
| D9 | Private source repo, public deploy repo `beltworks-play` on GitHub Pages | GitHub Pages isn't available for private repos on the current plan |
| D10 | Entity windows move items with `Insert {entity, item, count}` and `Take {entity, slot, count}` commands, naming entities by id (ids are never reused), instead of Factorio's cursor stack | There is no cursor stack yet; two commands cover every M1 transfer, stay deterministic, and are checked for reach in the simulation. A cursor stack can come with the M5 UI work |
| D11 | In an entity window, the same clicks move stacks both ways, into the entity (a click on an inventory stack) and out of it (a click on one of its slots): a click or shift-click moves the whole stack, a right click half of it (rounded up), a ctrl-click (⌘-click) or middle click one item. Revised in #36, which added taking half or one and moving one; at first only "insert all" and "insert half" existed | Without a cursor stack (D10) a click has to say how much it moves. Shift-click is the whole stack, as in Factorio; a single item is what's needed to share the 10 starting coal between all the starting machines |
| D12 | The burner mining drill is rotatable: it drops onto the tile in front of the left half of its front edge, whichever way it faces | Drills have to feed belts, furnaces and chests on any side; the drop tile is the same relative to the drill in every direction |
| D13 | Drills (and a hand's `Insert`) put items on the middle of a belt through the generic item hook: right lane first, the left lane when the right has no room there. Inserters drop onto the far lane | One hook serves every producer, with no belt-specific code in the drill. Unlike Factorio, a drill does not pick its lane from its side of the belt, so a lightly fed belt carries mostly on its right lane; once the right lane backs up the left fills, and drills saturating a belt still deliver exactly 15 items/s (`tests/sim/belts.test.ts`). Revisit if lane-specific builds need it (splitters, M3) |
| D14 | Waiting entities sleep, subscribed to the one entity they wait on, and are woken by notifications; a belt line only notifies its watched belts near the stretch of a lane that changed | Drills blocked on a belt and inserters waiting for items cost nothing per tick, which the 10k/50k targets (M4) need. Belts nobody watches pay nothing for notifications |
| D15 | An inserter with an empty fuel slot takes one unit of fuel from its source for itself (the optional rule from #17) | An inserter next to coal starts on its own, without a GUI step |
| D16 | Benchmarks: each scene runs 3 untimed and 5 measured runs of 600 warm-up and 3 600 timed ticks, and the run with the median mean is reported; the baseline comes from the CI runner's artifact, and regressions over 20% only warn | A 1k-scene tick takes microseconds: V8 warm-up, GC pauses and the mixed CPU pool of `ubuntu-latest` swing single runs by tens of percent, so a blocking check would be noise (docs/TOOLING.md, "Benchmarks") |
| D17 | A new game starts with a kit of belts, inserters, drills, furnaces, chests and coal (`src/data/start.ts`) | Makes the M1 loop reachable without hand-crafting everything first; the crafting queue arrived in the same milestone |
| D18 | Trees are a per-chunk layer of the world (not entities): generated from forest noise on grass outside a 48-tile starting radius, felled whole by hand-mining for 4 wood, blocking placement but not walking | Thousands of trees per forest would bloat the entity tables and the occupancy index; as world data they cost one byte per tile, are hashed like resources (only modified chunks), and are drawn in the baked chunk texture. Walking through them keeps movement simple; revisit if forests should block the player |
| D19 | The M2 power entities exist as prototype stubs (placeable placeholders) before they get behavior, and copper cable (made in twos) is not auto-queued as an intermediate | The data issue lands in parallel with the electric-network, generator and consumer issues, which add the tables; the crafting planner only auto-queues recipes that make exactly one unit, so the queue can refund cancelled crafts without tracking leftovers |
| D20 | M2 is Power and M3 Production chains and research (swapped from the original plan) | Assemblers and labs are electric, so production chains and research can't be built without poles, generators and the power solver; the electric inserter moves to M2 with them |
| D21 | An electric entity is tied to the pole with the lowest id among those whose supply area covers any of its tiles, and belongs to that pole's network; poles wire to every pole in reach, with no limit on wires | Pole ids never change, so merging or splitting networks moves an entity along with its pole and never needs a rescan; only placing or removing a pole nearby (or placing or rotating the entity) can re-tie it. Two small poles that cover the same tile are always within wire reach of each other, so the choice only matters for entities wider than 4 tiles. No wire limit keeps connections a pure function of positions, which the renderer reuses for the ghost's wire preview |
| D22 | Solver rounding: satisfaction is `floor(supply × 65536 / demand)` capped at 65536 (1 when there is supply and no demand); producers together deliver exactly `min(supply, demand)`, shared in proportion to their maximum output with cumulative rounding; a consumer gets `floor(demand × satisfaction / 65536)` | Integer-only and never creates energy. The producers' shares add up to the load exactly, so fuel burnt matches energy delivered; the consumers' rounding loses less than 1 J per consumer per tick |
| D23 | The starting kit carries 10 small electric poles (appended, so the other hotbar keys stay put) | Trees grow only outside the 48-tile starting radius (D18) and poles also need copper cable, so a new game could not try power without a long detour; the kit keeps the M2 loop and its E2E tests reachable from the spawn, like D17 did for M1 |
| D24 | The starting kit also carries a boiler and two steam engines (appended after the poles: hotbar keys 8 and 9) | Together they take 69 iron plates (16 of them as gears) and a furnace to craft, a long detour before a new game can try power; like D23, this keeps the M2 loop and its E2E tests reachable from the spawn |
| D25 | Steam without fluids: a boiler feeds the engines lined up end to end in front of its output side (each engine fed by at most one boiler, the lowest id); each tick, at a `powerPrepare` hook that runs before the solver, a boiler's supply (`min(30 000, buffer + one fuel unit)`) is shared out among its connected engines in the order steam reaches them, each taking up to 15 000 J | The solver asks every producer for its maximum before it draws from any, so engines reporting 15 000 each would promise more than a boiler with three engines has; allocating once per tick in a generic hook keeps the solver unchanged and the supply exact. Chaining engines end to end (as in Factorio) is the only way to give one boiler three engines with a 2-tile output side, and keeps parallel boiler rows from feeding each other's engines. A greedy split (rather than an even one) is simplest and only matters when a boiler has more engines than it can run |
| D26 | Electric consumers ask for the energy they are about to use, and only work on ticks it was granted: a drill 1 500 J while on its active list with somewhere to put a held unit, else 0; an inserter 220 J while on its active list, else its 7 J idle drain (whenever it is connected, also while asleep). Progress is in 1/65536 ticks and advances by the satisfaction per tick, stopping at the end of a unit or swing (no carry-over). `NoPower` machines stay on their active list. A consumer woken after the power step makes its choice (picks up, drops) at once but moves from the next tick | Demand from sleeping or blocked drills would lower everyone's satisfaction and make generators burn fuel for nothing; a drill blocked on a busy belt is woken every tick. Granting keeps energy conserved: nothing runs on energy the producers did not deliver, at the cost of one tick of delay for inserters woken by belts or drills (they still grab the item, so none is missed). The drain needs no wake-ups because the power step asks every connected consumer each tick anyway. Satisfaction can change on any tick without any entity changing, so waiting for power can't be a subscription; polling unpowered machines costs a few lookups per tick. Dropping the overshoot keeps "progress 0" meaning "start of a unit" as for burner drills, and gives exactly 120/240 ticks per unit at satisfaction 1 and 1/2 |
| D27 | The starting kit also carries 2 electric mining drills (hotbar key 0, the last one free) and 4 electric inserters (appended after them, so in the inventory past the hotbar) | They need electronic circuits, so a new game could not place one without a long crafting detour; the kit makes them testable end to end with the kit's boiler and engines, like D23 and D24 did for poles and steam power. The hotbar has no key left, so the inserters go in the inventory, and a new game's first mined items land past the hotbar too. Revised in #42: they are placed by clicking them in the inventory window (D28) |
| D28 | Any inventory slot can be selected for building: a click on a placeable item in the inventory window (E) selects its slot, and `Q` with nothing selected picks the item of the entity under the cursor (the first slot holding it, so the hotbar comes first; the ghost takes the entity's direction). Clicks select in the inventory window and move stacks in an entity window's copy of the inventory (D11); the two are never open together. A selection past the hotbar follows its item: it moves to another slot holding it when its slot runs out, and is dropped when none does; hotbar selections stay on their slot whatever it holds, as before. No key assigns an item to a hotbar slot | The selection stays a slot index, so the hotbar, the ghost and `Place` (which takes an item id) work unchanged and no sim change is needed. Following the item keeps a selected stack usable as it empties and refills elsewhere, without a later mined item that lands in the emptied slot becoming the selection. Hotbar assignment would need a sim command to move stacks between slots (and the hotbar to stop being "the first 10 slots"), so it waits for hotbar management in M5 |
| D29 | Dev and test builds take a starting kit from a `?kit=name:count,...` URL parameter (`startingKitFromUrl` in `src/testHook.ts`, sent in `init`); production builds don't contain the parser and always use `STARTING_KIT` | The M2 acceptance test has to overload a network: one steam engine (900 kW) runs 10 electric drills (90 kW each), so it takes 11 or more, and the kit carries 2 (D27); crafting the rest from ore by hand (circuits, gears, plates) is a long detour that has nothing to do with power. The kit replaces only what the player starts with: everything is still built with real mouse and keyboard input, and the simulation checks every command as usual. The bench scenes size their builders' kits the same way (`SimOptions.startingKit`) |
| D30 | The power panel's 60 s graph lives on the main thread (`PowerHistory`, sampled from snapshots, one averaged sample per simulated second, 60 per network), not in the simulation; the per-type totals come from a network detail the worker computes only while a pole is inspected; the panel needs no reach and shares the `inspect` slot with the entity GUI. The UI stays plain DOM (plus one canvas) | History is display data: keeping it in the simulation would grow the state and the hash, and every client of a future lockstep game can sample its own. Sampling every network around the view (not just the open one) means a panel shows a minute of history as soon as it opens. Snapshots only carry entities around the view, so totals over a whole network need the worker; scanning the network's members costs nothing while no panel is open. Reading a network moves no items, so unlike the entity GUI (D10) it has no reason to require reach. The panel fit in a few hundred lines of plain DOM, so Q1 stays open until the M3 tech tree |
| D31 | Hand-crafting still doesn't auto-queue intermediates from recipes that make more than one unit (copper cable makes 2; D19 stands), revisited after the #31 review | A cable craft for a circuit would make one cable too many. Handing that surplus to the player needs a new per-entry column (the units an intermediate entry makes beyond what its craft uses, given to the inventory when the entry finishes, and blocking the queue like any output that doesn't fit) and a refund rule for cancelled groups that counts the surplus of their remaining intermediates: a change to the crafting state, its hash and the determinism scenario, out of scope for the M2 wrap-up (UI, bench, tests). Players craft cable first (the crafting window shows it); revisit with the M3 assembler and recipe UI, where the same surplus question comes up for machines |
| D32 | Assembling machines: a recipe is any non-smelting recipe (`isAssemblerRecipe`; the hand recipes, the machines' own included) that the player's force has unlocked (`isRecipeUnlocked(state, force, recipe)` in `src/sim/research/unlocks.ts`, everything until research; one force for now). Slots are sized from the recipe (inputs in recipe order, then outputs) and only store counts; an input slot takes up to 2× the recipe's amount from inserters **and by hand**, an output slot up to a stack. `SetRecipe {entity, recipe}` (0 clears) needs reach and refunds the slots plus the ingredients of a craft under way to the player, and is ignored if they don't all fit (like `Remove`; nothing spills). Changing a recipe calls a new `entityReconfigured` hook, which wakes the inserters around the machine. Electric consumers under D26: `energyPerTick` while on the active list with a craft under way or one it can start, else 0 (no idle drain); progress in 1/65536 ticks, no carry-over, so a craft takes exactly `ceil(ticks × den / num)` ticks at full power (crafting speed 1/2 and 3/4) and twice that at half power | One table class for both tiers (they differ only in data). A uniform input limit keeps the `insert` hook source-agnostic and deterministic; Factorio lets a hand fill a whole stack, which can come with the M5 cursor stack. Refusing a recipe change whose refund doesn't fit keeps items from vanishing without inventing a spill-on-ground mechanic. An inserter waiting for something its target accepts subscribes to its source, so without the hook a new recipe would leave it asleep. The machines can make cable (2 per craft) without the D31 surplus question: their products go to an output slot, not into another craft. The picker gets its options from the worker (the entity detail lists the recipes `isRecipeUnlocked` allows), so research (#48) only has to implement the hook |
| D33 | Fast and long-handed inserters are `ElectricInserterTable`s built from their own prototypes, and the iron chest a `ChestTable` built from its prototype: every per-type number (swing ticks, working energy, drain, reach, slots) lives in `src/data/entities.ts`, and the inserter system steps every inserter table in type id order. A long-handed inserter reaches exactly 2 tiles each way (not 1 and 2): it ignores whatever stands next to it, and its `entityPlaced`/`entityRotated` wake-ups look 2 tiles away. Swing time is per type with no extra arm-length rule; the arm position stays in 1/65536 ticks with no overshoot carried over (D26), so the shared M3 numbers give exactly 26 and 50 ticks per item at satisfaction 1 and twice that at 1/2 | One code path for every inserter keeps sleep, wake-ups and belt lanes identical across types, and new tiers are data only. The long-handed inserter's 333 J/tick is 19.98 kW, the nearest integer to the shared 20 kW. Taking the table's type from the prototype means a new chest or inserter type is one registry line |
| D34 | Art: pre-rendered Blender sprites (settles Q4). Every model is a procedural `bpy` script under `art/blender/` (no .blend files, no third-party models or textures), rendered headless with Cycles from an orthographic camera 50° above the south (stretched so the ground grid stays square), one warm key light from the upper left and a sky, at 64 texels per tile; shadows are a separate pass drawn into one faded layer; moving parts are separate sprite layers. `npm run art` renders and packs the atlases into `public/sprites/`, which are committed: CI never runs Blender. The renderer falls back to the placeholder shapes for anything without a model (docs/ART.md) | 3D renders give readable, consistent industrial machines with real light and shadow, which neither pixel art nor flat vectors would at this level of detail without an artist; models as code are reviewable, reproducible and easy to restyle together (one camera, one light, one palette). 64 texels per tile keeps 2× zoom sharp. Rendering only the moving part per frame (holdouts hide it behind the body) keeps atlases small, and the same layers carry inserter arms, belts and terrain later. Committing the atlases keeps CI and contributors free of a Blender install; a full re-render takes minutes, reproduces the pixels to within 1/255, and is only needed when a model changes |
| D35 | Belt tiers are belt speeds (8 / 16 / 24 subtiles/tick, spacing 64): only same-speed belts merge into a line; a line feeding a belt of another tier hands items on at that belt's line start, lane by lane, like a loop. An item at a line's end is handed on in the same tick it arrives, and the item behind moves on with the rest of the tick's movement; a side-loaded item may enter the target up to one tick of the target's movement behind the side-load point; across a hand-over onto another line's start the spacing holds too (a lane's first item stays 64 behind the target's last item, and a handed item keeps its leftover movement, scaled to the target's speed); a lane blocked by a lane that moved this tick or the last stays awake; a rebuild restores the spacing across such a hand-over (items it pushes past the target's start move into the feeding lane, which is pushed back), and items left past a start still hold the feeding lane 64 behind them | Keeps every lane at one speed, so the O(1) gap stepping is unchanged; the hand-over is the loop's, already tested. Without carrying the leftover movement over, express items (24 does not divide 64) would leave every 3 ticks instead of every 2⅔, 40 items/s instead of 45; for the same reason a lane fed at a fixed point (a side-load) would fill every 3 ticks, and a blocked lane that slept would be woken a tick late (woken lanes run from the next tick), losing up to a fifth of the rate at boundaries and side-loads (6.8/s instead of 7.5 even yellow onto yellow). Staying awake costs one O(1) step per tick only while the lane it feeds moves; keying it to movement rather than to being on the active list lets a jammed ring of lines of different tiers fall asleep (they would otherwise keep each other awake forever). The carry-over changes when yellow items reach a loop's or side-load's end by a tick in some cases (new determinism hash) |
| D36 | Underground belts: the entrance and exit are one entity type per tier; a new one is an exit when an entrance of its tier behind it (the nearest same-tier underground on that axis, within reach) would pair with it, else an entrance; any same-tier underground on the axis in between blocks a pair, perpendicular ones and other tiers don't. `Rotate` reverses a pair (entrance and exit swap). The tiles between count as belt length (entries of 0 in the line) and the items there belong to the exit. Side-loading an underground only passes the feeding lane on its open half (Factorio's lane filter) | Factorio's rules, which players know, and all of them a pure function of positions, so the renderer can preview the pairing with the same code. Opposite-facing same-tier undergrounds blocking keeps "nearest" unambiguous. Putting the hidden tiles in the line keeps positions at 256 per index, so nothing else in the belt code changed; giving the tunnel to the exit means removing the entrance spills nothing and removing the exit refunds the tunnel, both conserving items |
| D37 | One entity table can hold several types (`EntityTypeTable.types`); the belt table holds all six belt types | Lines, side-loads and hand-overs run across tiers and between belts and undergrounds, so they need one table; registering it per type keeps every other lookup (`entities.table(type)`, item hooks) unchanged |
| D38 | Research: a lab researches one unit at a time, taking one of each of the technology's packs when it starts and finishing after the technology's `ticksPerUnit` working ticks at full power (progress in 1/65536 ticks, no carry-over, D26); a unit counts for the technology it was started for, even after the player switches research, and labs never start more units than the current research has left. Progress is kept per technology (units researched); a completed technology stops being the current research (no queue). Labs take every science pack type (one stack each) whatever is researched, refund their packs and the unit under way when removed, and give nothing to inserters. Recipes no technology unlocks are available from the start (all M1/M2 recipes, the lab, the automation science pack, the iron chest); hand crafting (intermediates included), `SetRecipe` and the recipe picker follow `isRecipeUnlocked`, but placing a locked item you already hold is allowed. Dev and test builds take `?research=name,...` (or `all`), like `?kit=` (D29). The determinism scenario's random `SetResearch` commands are rare (1 in 8 of their slot) so its labs get somewhere | Whole units make exact timings simple: one lab researches `u` units in `u × ticksPerUnit` ticks, `n` labs in `ceil(u / n) × ticksPerUnit`, twice that at half power (`tests/sim/research.test.ts`). Letting a started unit finish for its own technology means switching research never wastes packs, without tracking partly used packs. A per-technology "units in progress" count (derived from the labs, not hashed) keeps several labs from overshooting the unit count. Accepting every pack keeps a lab's inserters from depending on the current research. Lab-to-lab chaining by inserters and a research queue can come later. Placing a locked item is harmless (it had to be crafted or given) and keeps test kits (D29) working |
| D39 | The tech tree panel (and every UI so far) stays plain DOM, no Preact (settles Q1) | The panel is about 450 lines: cards laid out once at fixed positions (columns by depth) with SVG links, then only attributes, text and bar widths change per snapshot, behind a key so nothing is touched when nothing changed. That is the pattern of the entity, power and crafting panels; a virtual DOM would add a dependency and a JSX build step for no measurable gain at ~10 nodes. Revisit if M5's settings, save and blueprint screens need nested, stateful forms |
| D40 | Logistics sprites (belts, inserters, poles) go into a second atlas, `logistics`, next to `entities`; the game loads and merges both. A belt is three sprites: a moving surface shared by every tier (16 frames over one slat pitch, the frame picked from how far the belt has moved since tick 0, not from a clock), its side frames in the tier color, and an underground's hood above the items. An inserter is a static base plus 32 pre-rendered arm angles shared by every direction, the nearest one turned the rest of the way in the game, drawn in the y-sorted building layer with its held item at the model's `hand` and a faint shadow of its arm sprite (black, moved along the light by the arm's height). Belts are drawn north to south, so each covers the south end face of the belt north of it | A second atlas keeps the logistics art out of the buildings' files, so models of both families can be re-rendered (and reviewed) separately. Sharing the surface across tiers and splitting off the static frames cuts the belt frames three-fold (the surface is most of a belt's texels); driving it by distance makes the slats move exactly with the items at every tier and stay put when the game pauses. Pre-rendered angles keep the arm lit correctly from every side; turning a sprite by under 6° is invisible and makes the swing smooth without hundreds of frames. Sorting inserters with the buildings fixes arms drawing over the top of a machine behind them |
| D41 | Rendered terrain is baked per chunk on the GPU: a ground shader blends four seamless 8×8-tile ground textures (Blender, periodic 4D noise) through a per-chunk splat texture of tile types with noise-pushed soft edges, then ore decals (3 richness levels × 3 variants), decor, and trees (6 variants, D18 unchanged: still world data in the baked texture) with their shadows are drawn on top, including those of neighbouring tiles within 2 tiles. The texture has 32 texels per tile at 1× (4 MiB; at most 16 on software renderers) and halves with the zoom; only chunks near the view are baked, and textures further than 24 tiles are freed. The power entities, terrain and icons get their own atlases (`power`, `terrain`, `icons`, as D40 does for logistics). Item icons are 52² renders loaded at resolution 4; the lettered icons stay as the fallback and an option | One texture per chunk keeps panning as cheap as before (one sprite per chunk on screen) while letting terrain types blend and trees overlap neighbouring tiles, which tile sprites would need thousands of draws for. Baking on the GPU needs no pixel buffers or blending on the main thread; zoom-dependent density keeps memory per screen constant (texel for pixel at 1×, a quarter of the texels at half the zoom), where a fixed 64 texels per tile would take 16 MiB per chunk. Blending in a shader needs no transition art per pair of terrains. Separate atlases keep parallel art work (logistics, power) off one sheet and let one group be re-rendered alone. Icons at resolution 4 change no layer that draws them. Test builds draw the flat placeholder terrain unless a spec passes `?art=1` (the art, render and screenshot specs do; docs/TOOLING.md): on CI's software renderer the rendered terrain's bakes doubled the end-to-end run, and the functional specs don't look at the ground |
| D42 | Dev and test builds can speed up the simulation: the test hook's `setTimeScale(n)` (an integer 1–16) sends the worker a `setTimeScale` message, and the worker loop then runs `n` ticks per 1/60 s of wall time, in one batch (one snapshot) per normal step. Production builds reject the message (the check is compiled in) and never send it; the default is 1, where the loop is unchanged. E2E specs use it only while the player stands still and waits (`tests/e2e/timeScale.ts`) | The longest specs spent most of their time waiting on simulated time: the M2 acceptance test measured three 20 s drill rates, research waited 30 s, crafting mined 25 s of stone, each at real speed on CI's slow software-rendered Chromium. Ticks are the same at any speed, so the measurements (in ticks) and the game state stay exact and the input is still real; only the wall time changes. Batching per normal step keeps the main thread at one snapshot per frame. The player also walks `n` times faster, which is why walking and building stay at normal speed. |
| D43 | Terrain and icon readability (#70). The ground shader samples the splat halfway between bilinear and a cubic B-spline (four bilinear taps), over a splat with a two-tile border, and pushes it with a broad and a fine noise field, so shores no longer step along the grid at low zoom. Ore patches get a noisy outline from a second per-chunk splat texture of ore: the ground pass stains the soil inside it, and a second pass of the same shader, a quad per patch-edge tile (listed by the plan), covers the decals outside it; ore decals are jittered by up to 1/8 tile. Zoomed in from 1.5× on a real GPU, the chunks within a tile of the view get a second, 64-texel detail texture (16 MiB) over their base one, baked one per frame only in frames without a base bake and freed beyond 4 tiles from the view or below 1.5×; software renderers never get them (`?texels=64` allows them in tests). Item icons get shape cues next to color: distinct ore silhouettes, tier chevrons on belts and undergrounds, side-on inserters | Smooth interpolation fixes the staircase where it comes from (the per-tile data) at every zoom, at four texture reads; the outline pass costs only the edge tiles, so baking stays cheap on software renderers. A separate detail layer leaves the base textures, their budget, their memory bounds and panning exactly as before (D41), bounds the extra memory by what is on screen and frees it without a re-bake; a single texture per chunk at 64 texels would cost 16 MiB for every chunk kept around the view. Icons read at 13 px, where color alone fails for color-blind players and similar tiers |
| D44 | Splitters: a splitter is one belt-table entity (2×1 across the way items move; `Rotate` reverses it on the same tiles) whose halves are transport lines of their own, one belt long, that never merge with the belts around them: the belt behind a half hands on to its start like at a tier boundary, and a half hands on to the start of the line in front of it (straight on or as a curve; a splitter never side-loads, and nothing side-loads it). A half's first item goes to the output its lane's alternation bit picks (per splitter and lane, hashed), else to the other one; going to the picked one flips the bit. Going to the other output, the item moves over onto the other half's lane (into the nearest free slot, up to 128 back), marked so it goes straight on from there, and only while that output is open. While a half has items its output can't take, the other half's lane closes up to 128 instead of 64 (`MERGE_SPACING`), leaving a slot behind each of its own items. Halves step after every other lane. Input and output priorities are out of scope (no settings) | Sending items across through the other half (rather than onto its output directly) makes every side of a splitter carry at most one belt of its tier and keeps the spacing across both boundaries with the ordinary rules, whatever the splitter, input and output tiers; a first version that put them straight onto the other output let a yellow splitter put 22.5/s onto one side and starved one input when the output was slower (review of #68). The gap-array lanes can only close gaps, so a fair merge has to keep the free slot from the half's start: an item packed 64 behind can't make room in time, and a straight-first rule (as for side-loads) would starve the other input; the slot is kept behind the half's own items, so the input feeding it isn't held up. Marks keep an item that crossed from crossing back (a ping-pong between halves), except off a blocked output. Lines of their own keep lanes O(1) and reuse the hand-over, rebuild (`settle`) and sleep rules as they are. Stepping halves last means a half decides with the outputs where they end the tick. The cost: an item crossing over may land up to half a tile behind where it was, and a half joining a merge passes the items already packed at 64 before the turns start. A drill's or hand's `Insert` uses the left half first |
